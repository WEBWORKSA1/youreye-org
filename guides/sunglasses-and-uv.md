## Why UV matters for eyes

Ultraviolet light from the sun can damage the eye's surface, cornea and lens. Over years, unprotected exposure is linked to cataract, growths on the eye such as pterygium, and cancers of the eyelid. Intense short exposure, for example on snow or water, can cause *photokeratitis*, a painful sunburn of the cornea.

## What to look for

- **100% UVA and UVB protection, or UV400.** The American Academy of Ophthalmology says both labels mean the same thing.
- **Coverage.** Larger lenses and wraparound styles block light from the sides.
- **Good optics.** Look through the lenses at a straight line, such as a door frame, and move them; the line should not wobble.

## Myths

| Myth | Fact |
| --- | --- |
| Darker lenses protect more | Darkness does not show UV protection. Dark lenses without UV filters can let more UV in by widening the pupil |
| Polarized means UV-safe | Polarization cuts glare from water and roads; check the UV label separately |
| Only sunny days matter | UV gets through clouds and haze |
| Contacts with UV filter are enough | They do not cover the whole eye and lids; wear sunglasses too |

## Children

Children's lenses let more UV reach the retina, and they spend a lot of time outdoors. Choose impact-resistant polycarbonate sunglasses with UV400 and a brimmed hat.

## Solar eclipses

Ordinary sunglasses do not protect you when looking at the sun. Use certified eclipse glasses or viewers that meet the ISO 12312-2 standard, or watch indirectly with a pinhole projector. Looking at the sun can burn the retina permanently.
